## 1. One High Ball. Two Opposite Answers.

**Belief shift:** high bounce alone does not tell you to advance or retreat.
Read where a usable contact will be relative to your current position and time.
Neutral rally focus: no jump forehands, drive volleys or technique diagnosis.
Comfortable contact is an authored range for this player, not a universal height.

### Decision 1 — moving forward is available

Setup: A slower ball lands short. You are balanced and have time to move.

Question: Where should you meet this ball?

- A. Stay back and wait for its drop
- B. Move forward into a comfortable contact
- C. Move back to let the bounce fall
- D. Stay level and meet it higher

Best: B.

Evidence: same-camera side-oblique view shows the bounce well inside the baseline,
the player behind it, and a reachable comfortable contact before the ball rises
too high. Three cues: landing location, player ready, available contact window.
No green target or suggested footstep appears before the answer.

Payoff: Moving forward uses the time without letting the ball dictate your contact.

Why: A/C surrender ground despite available preparation time. D accepts a higher
contact unnecessarily. Advancing is supported here, not prescribed for every lob.

### Decision 2 — the earlier window is no longer reachable

Setup: The next ball lands deep. Your recovery leaves you late for the rising contact.

Question: Which movement preserves a usable contact?

- A. Move forward to take it earlier
- B. Hold ground and strike above shoulder height
- C. Move sideways while keeping the same depth
- D. Create space behind the rising bounce

Best: D.

Evidence: deeper bounce and player still recovering; the early-contact window
passes before the player can reach it. There is usable court behind. Cues show
deep bounce, recovery still underway, and the rising ball's relation to the body.

Payoff: Giving ground restores space when the earlier contact is already gone.

Why: A requires time this player no longer has. B accepts an awkward height; C
does not resolve the depth problem. A skilled, prepared early take could reverse
the answer: the animation must make this particular late arrival unambiguous.

### Decision 3 — transfer: the player has already created the space

Setup: You start farther back. This high bounce now reaches your comfortable strike zone.

Question: What adjustment does this ball need?

- A. Hold depth and make small spacing steps
- B. Retreat farther before starting the swing
- C. Advance quickly to shorten the rally
- D. Move sideways to strike above shoulder height

Best: A.

Evidence: new trajectory, player already behind the bounce and prepared. Cues
show current depth, bounce position and the ball entering a body-relative contact
range. It must not simply say STAY or repeat the answer in a caption.

Payoff: You already have the space. Another retreat would give away more court.

Why: B applies the previous movement without reading the new starting position.
C/D alter an already usable contact. “High means back” must fail this transfer.

Memory: Find the contact, not the height.

Court experiment: During a cooperative rally, notice bounce depth and your starting
position before choosing your adjustment. Observe whether contact feels less rushed;
do not treat this as a prescription to force every ball to waist height.

## 2. Your Approach Shapes Your First Volley

**Belief shift:** inspect the situation your approach creates before blaming the
hands. This is an approach-to-volley consequence lesson, NOT Fix the Culprit.
Proposed rename removes the unsupported implication that a particular miss began
earlier. “Shapes” is conditional; even a good approach can meet a good pass.

### Decision 1 — prepare the next contact

Setup: You are balanced on a short forehand. Their recovery leaves the line available.

Question: Which approach best prepares your first volley?

- A. Drive short through the middle and follow
- B. Angle short across court and follow
- C. Drive deep down the line and follow
- D. Float centrally and close behind it

Best: C.

Evidence: right-handed near player on forehand side; opponent recovering from the
opposite side. Cues show usable approach contact, receiver's recovery and available
depth. Show singles boundaries and meaningful margins, not a sideline winner.

Payoff: The deeper approach keeps their contact back while you move into position.

Why: A/D let the receiver contact more comfortably inside the court. B provides a
shorter crosscourt passing geometry in this setup. C is not a universal command to
approach down the line; selected consequence is one plausible reply, not a forecast.

### Decision 2 — direction alone is insufficient

Setup: You use the same line, but land short. They step inside to pass.

Question: What should change in the next approach?

- A. Keep the landing and close another stride
- B. Add depth while keeping useful margin
- C. Keep the landing and add more pace
- D. Keep the length and use a sharper angle

Best: B.

Evidence: replay the short approach, receiver stepping in, and the arriving low
first volley. Stage those events sequentially; do not display the whole rally at
once. Cues are short bounce, receiver inside, volley below net level.

Payoff: Changing the landing changes the passing situation before your volley begins.

Why: A changes your location but not their comfortable pass. C can also remove
your closing time. D opens an angle without addressing the short landing. No
claim that these options always lose or that depth guarantees a high volley.

### Decision 3 — transfer to the other wing and opponent position

Setup: You approach from the backhand side. They wait near that sideline, leaving space across.

Question: Which approach creates the better first-volley situation?

- A. Repeat the line into their ready position
- B. Drop short along their occupied sideline
- C. Drive short through the central lane
- D. Play deep across and follow its direction

Best: D.

Evidence: near player's balanced backhand contact and far opponent displaced
toward that down-the-line lane, with a generous crosscourt target. The defender's
movement and passer position must justify the crossover from the first decision.

Payoff: This time, depth across court moves them away from their comfortable pass.

Why: A applies “down the line” without checking who is waiting there. B/C allow
a forward contact. D earns movement with depth; the approacher follows the new
ball line and splits at opponent contact rather than teleporting to net centre.

Memory: Your approach builds the volley.

Court experiment: In approach-and-volley practice, notice the opponent's contact
position and your first-volley height. Compare these with your approach landing,
without treating any single missed volley as proof of a technical or tactical fault.

## 3. Every Serve Teaches the Returner

**Belief shift:** the same landing coordinate need not retain the same tactical
effect when the receiver moves. Adapt to evidence; do not change for novelty.
The player has comparable command of the shown first-serve targets. This is not
second-serve technique advice, an underarm-serve tutorial or a prediction of minds.

### Decision 1 — a useful pattern remains available

Setup: Two firm serves cramped their swing. They return to the same starting spot.

Question: Which target still offers the same pressure?

- A. Repeat the firm central target
- B. Move the landing toward the T
- C. Use the wider service-box target
- D. Soften the serve into the centre

Best: A.

Evidence: two successive contacts, one at a time, show limited racket-body space;
receiver then returns to the same location. Cues show the first cramped contact,
the repeated contact, and the unchanged starting position. No claim of certainty.

Payoff: Their position still lets the same serve restrict the swing.

Why: B/C can be legitimate variations but do not preserve this demonstrated
pressure. D changes the time available. The question asks for the same pressure,
not whether any other serve could win the point.

### Decision 2 — the coordinate is no longer the body

Setup: They shift sideways. The same landing now gives their forehand room to swing.

Question: How can you restore the cramped contact?

- A. Keep the target and serve harder
- B. Repeat the target with less pace
- C. Shift the target toward their new body line
- D. Move the target farther from their body

Best: C.

Evidence: previous and new receiver positions shown sequentially against the same
fixed serve path. Their new forehand spacing is rendered with correct handedness.
The adjusted body target must still lie safely in the diagonal service box.

Payoff: The returner moved. The target must move to recreate the same pressure.

Why: A/B retain the opened contact lane; pace alone is not the same spatial
problem. D can stretch rather than jam, but that is a different tactical effect.
This narrowly framed question avoids pretending the body serve is always best.

### Decision 3 — transfer: do not invent an adjustment

Setup: From the other service court, two returns stay cramped. Their starting position holds.

Question: What does the current evidence support?

- A. Widen the target to change the pattern
- B. Keep the target that still crowds them
- C. Shift inward to change their contact
- D. Slow the serve to change its rhythm

Best: B.

Evidence: legal ad-court geometry, same receiver position across two serves and
visible close contact. Do not imply players automatically adapt after two serves.
Mirror court geometry intentionally; do not mirror the receiver's handedness.

Payoff: Keep useful pressure while it remains useful. Change when the evidence changes.

Why: A/C/D vary something without evidence this demonstrated advantage has gone.
The transfer tests the boundary to the adjustment learned in decision 2; it does
not promise that a predictable serve stays effective forever.

Memory: Read the returner, not the spot.

Court experiment: After a useful serve, notice where the receiver starts next
time and whether their contact stays cramped. Do not assume one good return
proves adaptation or invent a strict two-serve switching rule.

BODY—must support both crowded and free contact. Preserve net crossing, diagonal
bounce, post-bounce direction, receiver reach and natural serve/bounce audio.
