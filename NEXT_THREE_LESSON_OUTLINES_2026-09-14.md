## 19. The Second Serve You Let Escape

Belief shift: attacking a second serve can begin before you add racket pace.
Memory: **You can take time before you add pace.**
Court experiment: on a manageable second serve, compare a balanced earlier return
to your usual contact, retaining a generous target. Do not manufacture early
contact when the ball or your position makes it awkward.

### Decision 1 — notice the unused time

Situation: You wait for this second serve behind the baseline. Watch the forward contact available on the replay.

Question: What would test the unused time here?

- A. Move forward; keep useful pace and depth.
- B. Stay back; drive towards a wider target.
- C. Move back; add height through the middle.
- D. Stay back; shorten the return landing depth.

Best: A.
Payoff: The earlier, balanced contact brings this return back sooner.
Principle: You can change the time available before increasing return pace.
Why: This ball offers an accessible contact in front. Keeping a useful target and
pace tests that opportunity; changing angle or landing depth tests something else.

Evidence: (1) legal serve bounce, (2) reachable forward contact window and receiver
start, (3) later contact and arrival. Replay A against the original on one clock,
holding the incoming serve, outgoing speed and target fixed. Other choices must
change actual player position or return target, not captions alone. Show the
server's same movement clock if making any recovery claim.

Boundary: this is a controlled first test, not a universal optimum. It does not
prove equal execution risk, a guaranteed weak next ball or that pace cannot work.

### Decision 2 — distinguish contact time from flight time

Situation: Watch two returns from contact to landing. Compare their arrival clocks.

Question: Why does the controlled return arrive first here?

- A. The earlier strike saves more than pace recovers.
- B. The controlled return travels faster after racket contact.
- C. The controlled return uses a nearer landing target.
- D. The later return starts from a different serve.

Best: A.
Payoff: Extra pace does not recover all the time lost before this later contact.
Principle: Judge contact time and return flight together.
Why: The later return really travels faster. Its shorter flight still does not
offset its later start. Both returns use the same incoming serve and landing
target; sufficiently more pace could reverse this particular comparison.

Evidence: (1) same serve, (2) actual return contacts, (3) actual return landings.
Use neutral Return A / Return B labels before commitment; do not disclose their
pace, equality of targets or the explanation in the setup. The scene establishes these.
Use one elapsed reference from serve strike. The local proof currently shows
contacts at 1.20/1.58 seconds and landings at 2.15/2.36 seconds. These are authored
examples, not professional measurements. Importantly, the earlier return has the
LONGER outgoing flight (0.95 versus 0.78 seconds). Do not write that early contact
shortens both elapsed flight segments. The difference is net arrival time.

Editorial risk: B–D test observed causes, not tactical commands. Keep every option
in that same category. Do not let the clock turn this into arithmetic homework;
the visual comparison must make the time difference legible without calculation.

### Decision 3 — the first contact must be usable

Situation: From your starting position, the first forward contact you can reach is above your shoulder. Compare the available positions.

Question: Which response preserves a useful return here?

- A. Keep moving forward and shorten the return swing.
- B. Hold the baseline and drive the return faster.
- C. Give measured ground and meet the descending ball.
- D. Move farther forward and block through the middle.

Best: C, only if the reach and height evidence supports it.
Payoff: Giving ground lets this ball descend into a balanced strike.
Principle: Take useful time, not early contact at any cost.
Why: From this start, earlier low interception is unavailable. A measured retreat
reaches a manageable descending ball; shortening the swing does not remove the
height or travel problem shown in the alternatives.

Evidence: (1) actual receiver start, (2) ball height at earliest reachable forward
position, (3) descending contact space. Before the question, show available
windows neutrally, not the correct retreat or its payoff. Forward alternatives
must not acquire impossible speed. Hold retains the actual height problem.

Boundary: **a high or deep serve alone never proves retreat is right**. An earlier,
balanced interception may be excellent for another starting position/player.
This boundary remains an unbuilt visual gate, not something the current proof tests.

## 20. The Safe Second Serve Trap

Reframe the original mechanics brief as **tactical construction**. Do not diagnose
racket deceleration, infer spin from a curve, or claim comparative double-fault
probabilities from two legal examples. “Safe” describes the temptation to judge
only whether a serve lands in, not a measured execution-risk category.

Belief shift: judge the contact your second serve gives back, not only its bounce.
Memory: **In is the start of the assessment.**
Court experiment: after a second serve, notice the receiver's position and balance
at contact. Test a purposeful delivery you can execute; compare the resulting
contacts and your own errors over practice, not imagined accuracy percentages.

### Decision 1 — a legal serve can invite a forward strike

Situation: Both second serves land in. Watch the receiver's contact.

Question: What changes between these two return contacts?

- A. The ball arrives higher at the receiver's racket.
- B. The receiver meets one ball farther inside court.
- C. The receiver has less time before racket contact.
- D. The receiver must switch to the other wing.

Best: B.
Payoff: Both are legal; the shorter delivery gives this receiver a contact farther inside the court.
Principle: A legal bounce does not describe the contact it offers.
Why: Here the receiver can move forward on the shorter delivery. The deeper one
moves that same-height contact back. This comparison does not show serve-in
rates, a mechanical fault or a guaranteed attacking return.

Evidence: (1) serve contact, (2) legal diagonal bounce, (3) receiver contact. Same
receiver start, contact height and elapsed contact time in this first controlled
comparison. The proof changes depth AND flight velocity; do not claim equal
initial speed. Keep contact circles, starting ghost and bounce footprints in
reduced motion. Editorial gate: this opening is simple by design; the following
construction and reversal must supply the learning depth.

### Decision 2 — construct a different receiving problem

Situation: This receiver repeatedly steps in and drives the shorter second serve. Compare the contacts four deliveries offer.

Question: Which delivery most limits that forward drive here?

- A. Less depth with the original bouncing shape.
- B. Less pace with the original bounce depth.
- C. More pace with the original bounce depth.
- D. More depth with the higher bouncing shape.

Best: D, conditional on rendered contacts.
Payoff: This deeper, higher delivery removes the easy forward strike shown before.
Principle: Change the receiving contact, not just the word “safe”.
Why: The receiver handles the shorter alternatives comfortably in this scene.
The deeper, higher delivery changes that contact. It is the observed relationship,
not height or depth in isolation, that earns the choice.

Evidence: sequential neutral contact previews, one active ball, held contact
summaries. Every option must really change its named property. C must travel
faster; B must give more time. D must remove the shown forward contact without
manufacturing an impossible retreat. No cue may assert a weak return before it
occurs. This case is not yet built; do not extend the first proof's result to it.

Boundary: more pace could solve another receiver; high contact may suit another
player. No ball-flight animation can by itself diagnose racket-speed mechanics.

### Decision 3 — higher is not the new automatic answer

Situation: Another receiver handles the high backhand comfortably. Watch their contact against the other deliveries from the same starting position.

Question: Which delivery changes their comfortable contact most here?

- A. High again into the backhand hitting lane.
- B. Lower and wide towards the forehand sideline.
- C. Higher and short towards the same sideline.
- D. Slower and deep through the central lane.

Best: B, conditional on genuine lateral stretch.
Payoff: Here the lower wide delivery makes them reach; the higher ball did not.
Principle: Keep the receiving problem, not a favourite ball shape, as the objective.
Why: This receiver is balanced on the high backhand. The wide delivery changes
their reach; the other examples leave a manageable strike. Another starting
position or unreliable execution could change the call.

Evidence: right-handed high backhand and forehand sides correctly rendered;
identical receiver starts across candidates; legal service bounces; actual body
spacing, height and lateral travel. No invented weaker wing. No guaranteed
return direction, point win or claim that wide serving is inherently safer.

Boundary: a pattern outside the server's repertoire is not a useful prescription.
The footage does not show comparative execution reliability. This must not become
another moving-body-target exercise; Serve Adaptation already owns that lesson.

## 21. One Point Is a Clue

Belief shift: “They lobbed me” becomes “They lobbed from that contact”.
Memory: **Keep the conditions with the clue.**
Court experiment: after a surprise reply, remember the contact that produced it.
Compare similar contacts; start a separate observation when the conditions change.

### Decision 1 — retain a useful clue

Situation: Their lob sends you backwards. Replay the approach that came before it.
Question: What would test this clue?

- A. Track their lobs across the whole match.
- B. Compare replies from that deep backhand stretch.
- C. Track replies whenever you reach the net.
- D. Compare replies from every backhand they play.

Best: B.
Payoff: The lob is useful evidence when you keep the contact conditions attached.
Principle: One reply starts a question; comparable contacts help answer it.
Why: The surprise came from a particular contact. Comparing that situation tests
the clue; pooling every lob, approach or backhand mixes different opportunities.

Evidence: (1) deep approach bounce, (2) low backhand stretch behind the baseline,
(3) actual lob above volley reach. Neutral labels BOUNCE / CONTACT / REPLY.
Do not name a “lob position” as though the reply were inevitable.

### Decision 2 — separate comparable from pooled evidence

Situation: The next approaches bring several replies. Watch where each contact happens.
Question: Which pattern has the clearest support?

- A. Lobs whenever you move inside the court.
- B. Lobs from their backhand at any depth.
- C. Lobs from those deep, stretched backhand contacts.
- D. Passes when you approach their backhand side.

Best: C.
Payoff: The repeated lob belongs to this contact situation, not every approach.
Principle: A pattern is more useful when you know the situation that produced it.
Why: Comparable stretched backhand contacts repeatedly produced lobs. The balanced
backhand pass belongs to a different situation. Prepare for the observed option
without predicting the next reply.

Evidence: opening lob plus two subsequent lobs from comparable deep, stretched
backhands; one drive pass from a shorter, balanced backhand. Play sequentially
with one active ball. Preserve small contact snapshots, not only outgoing arrows.
Selecting an interpretation retains the evidence it groups and exposes what it
mixes or omits. The unlike backhand is essential: otherwise “backhand = lob” wins.

Boundary: these are authored observations, not research data or a magic
three-event threshold. An obvious physical constraint can justify acting at once.

### Decision 3 — the new contact does not inherit the old read

Situation: Your next approach lands shorter. Their forehand meets it comfortably inside the baseline.
Question: How should you prepare now?

- A. Retreat early to cover the expected lob.
- B. Keep closing to smother the next reply.
- C. Shade crosscourt before their racket meets ball.
- D. Set for either reply and read contact.

Best: D.
Payoff: Those earlier lobs do not establish a preference from this new contact.
Principle: Changed conditions can remove a pattern's relevance without proving it wrong.
Why: The earlier evidence came from stretched backhands behind the baseline. This
balanced forehand creates different options. Set your feet and read its flight
before choosing the passing or lob response.

Evidence: (1) shorter bounce, (2) opponent inside the baseline, (3) comfortable
forehand contact. Stop BEFORE the outgoing reply. Alternative player movements
must differ before contact; show available space without guaranteeing a winner.
Card, Focus and reduced motion must preserve the same historical comparison.
